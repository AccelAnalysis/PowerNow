# Evidence Notes and Sources

*Clarity Creates Speed* is a practical business book, not an academic textbook. The research below is included to support the major claims made in the manuscript without pretending that any single study proves the complete Power NOW framework.

## Part I Introduction — Planning, Clarity, and Execution

**Dvir, D., Raz, T., & Shenhar, A. J. (2003).** “An empirical analysis of the relationship between project planning and project success.” *International Journal of Project Management, 21*(2), 89–95.  
DOI: 10.1016/S0263-7863(02)00012-1

The study examined more than one hundred defense research-and-development projects. Project success was positively correlated with effort invested in requirements definition and technical specifications, while implementation of project-management processes and procedures was not itself associated with success in the same way. The manuscript uses this distinction to support **clarity over bureaucracy**.

**Serrador, P. (2013).** “The Impact of Planning on Project Success — A Literature Review.” *The Journal of Modern Project Management, 1*(2).

The literature review reported a consistent relationship between planning quality, project efficiency, and overall project success. The book uses this as support for the general proposition that planning quality matters while avoiding the stronger claim that more planning always produces faster results.

**Brinckmann, J., Grichnik, D., & Kapsa, D. (2010).** “Should entrepreneurs plan or just storm the castle? A meta-analysis on contextual factors impacting the business planning–performance relationship in small firms.” *Journal of Business Venturing, 25*(1), 24–40.  
DOI: 10.1016/j.jbusvent.2008.10.007

This meta-analysis synthesized 46 studies and 51 effect sizes. The overall relationship between business planning and firm performance was positive, but contextual factors mattered. The authors advocated a dynamic approach combining planning and learning. The manuscript uses that nuance to support **plan enough to act, then learn in motion**.

## Chapter 1 — Define the Win

**Locke, E. A., & Latham, G. P. (2002).** “Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: A 35-year odyssey.” *American Psychologist, 57*(9), 705–717.  
DOI: 10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705

Locke and Latham summarized decades of goal-setting research showing that, under appropriate conditions, specific and challenging goals generally outperform vague “do your best” goals. The book uses this work to support the practical value of defining a concrete win.

Related foundational review:

**Locke, E. A., Shaw, K. N., Saari, L. M., & Latham, G. P. (1981).** “Goal setting and task performance: 1969–1980.” *Psychological Bulletin, 90*(1), 125–152.  
DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.90.1.125

## Chapter 2 — Work That Moves the Needle

**Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001).** “Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching.” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27*(4), 763–797.  
DOI: 10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763

Across four experiments, alternating between tasks produced measurable switching-time costs, with greater costs as rule complexity increased. The manuscript uses this evidence to support reducing unnecessary interruption and protecting continuity around high-value work.

## Chapter 3 — The NOW Success Cycle

**Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006).** “Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-analysis of Effects and Processes.” *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38*, 69–119.  
DOI: 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1

The meta-analysis reviewed 94 independent tests and found a medium-to-large positive effect of implementation intentions on goal attainment (d = .65). Implementation intentions specify when, where, or under what condition an action will occur. The book uses this research to support pre-deciding the response and shrinking the gap between intention and action.

## Chapter 4 — The Big Three for Today

**Aeon, B., Faber, A., & Panaccio, A. (2021).** “Does time management work? A meta-analysis.” *PLOS ONE, 16*(1), e0245066.  
DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0245066

The meta-analysis included 158 studies, 490 effect sizes, and 53,957 participants. Time management was moderately related to job performance, academic achievement, and well-being and negatively related to distress. The authors describe time-management measures as capturing behaviors such as structuring, protecting, and adapting time.

The manuscript does **not** claim that “three” is a scientifically optimal number of daily priorities. The Big Three is presented as a practical constraint for applying broader evidence about prioritization and time structure.

## Editorial Research Standard for the Power NOW Series

Where research is used in future volumes, the manuscript should distinguish among:

1. **What a study directly found.**
2. **What practical inference the book draws from it.**
3. **What is an original Power NOW framework or heuristic.**

This distinction protects the credibility of the series and keeps evidence from being stretched beyond what the underlying research supports.
