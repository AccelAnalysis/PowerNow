# Chapter 3 — The NOW Success Cycle

Success is usually talked about like an event.

You “make it.”

You “arrive.”

The business finally takes off. The contract lands. The book gets published. The weight comes off. The degree gets finished. The relationship gets repaired.

We like the moment when the result becomes visible.

But visible success is almost never where the story begins.

Before the result, there was a start.

Before the result became reliable, something had to be sustained.

And before the result could be repeated, somebody had to understand what was working well enough to keep doing it.

That is the idea behind the **NOW Success Cycle**:

> **Start → Sustain → Succeed**

It is intentionally simple.

Not because meaningful goals are simple, but because complexity becomes easier to manage when you can answer one question:

> **Where am I in the process right now?**

A lot of frustration comes from treating every goal problem like the same problem.

It is not.

If you have not begun, you have a **Start** problem.

If you keep beginning but cannot maintain the right actions, you have a **Sustain** problem.

If the actions are working and producing the desired outcome, you have entered **Succeed**—and now your challenge is to recognize the result, protect it, learn from it, and eventually multiply it.

The phase changes the question.

And when the question becomes clearer, the next action usually does too.

---

## START: Turn Intention Into Motion

Start is the shortest word in the cycle and, sometimes, the hardest phase.

This is where an idea has to cross the line into the physical world.

You can want to:

- send the proposal,
- build the website,
- apply for the opportunity,
- make the sales call,
- register the business,
- write the chapter,
- create the offer,
- start walking,
- have the conversation.

Until something moves, all of that remains intention.

A Start action is not “work on the business.”

It is not “think about marketing.”

It is not “get serious about writing.”

A Start action is something observable.

> **Open the document and write the first paragraph.**

> **Send the first follow-up.**

> **Buy the domain.**

> **Call the first prospect.**

> **Put the first workout on the calendar and do it.**

The point of Start is not to finish the whole journey.

The point is to **change the condition from not-in-motion to in-motion**.

That distinction matters because our brains often make starting harder than it is by mentally loading the entire project into the first step.

“Write a book” sounds enormous.

“Write the opening paragraph” is executable.

“Build a sales pipeline” sounds enormous.

“Create the first five prospect records” is executable.

“Fix my finances” sounds enormous.

“Open the statements and list the balances” is executable.

Start is where possibility becomes evidence.

There is now something to react to, improve, measure, continue, or correct.

---

## SUSTAIN: Keep the Right Thing Moving

Starting feels powerful because it creates motion.

Sustaining is quieter.

It is also where a great many goals are won or lost.

You can send one proposal and still not have a business-development system.

You can work out once and still not have a fitness practice.

You can write one page and still not have a book.

You can follow up with one client and still not have a follow-up process.

Sustain asks:

> **What has to keep happening, at what rhythm, for this result to have a real chance?**

That might mean:

- five follow-ups each weekday,
- one meaningful product improvement each week,
- one hour of writing every morning,
- a weekly review of pipeline numbers,
- a fixed savings transfer each payday,
- a standing date night,
- a recurring customer check-in.

The goal is not to create a heroic burst.

It is to create a repeatable pattern.

This is where many people accidentally depend on emotion.

They sustain while the idea is exciting.

They sustain while people are watching.

They sustain while the launch is new.

Then life becomes ordinary again, and the action disappears.

A real sustain system has to survive some ordinary days.

It has to be small enough, clear enough, and protected enough that it still happens when nobody is cheering.

---

## SUCCEED: Recognize the Result

This sounds obvious, but people often fail to define what success will look like before they start.

So they can make progress and still feel behind.

Or worse, they can continue doing the same activity long after it has stopped producing value because they never defined the result that would tell them the cycle had worked.

Succeed is where the output becomes visible.

The proposal becomes a signed contract.

The follow-up becomes a sale.

The repeated writing becomes a finished manuscript.

The recurring process becomes a stable system.

The consistent saving becomes a funded reserve.

The repeated customer experience becomes referrals.

Succeed asks:

> **What evidence will tell me this worked?**

That evidence should connect back to the win you defined in Chapter 1.

It might be a number.

It might be a completed deliverable.

It might be a behavior that now happens reliably.

It might be a change in someone else's condition.

The important thing is that success is not just “I feel good about this.”

You should be able to point to something and say:

> **That is the result this cycle was supposed to produce.**

---

## Success Is Not the End of the Cycle

The arrow does not stop at Succeed.

A win creates new information.

You now know something you did not know when you began.

You know:

- what worked,
- what took longer than expected,
- what customers responded to,
- which part of the process broke,
- what you should repeat,
- what you should remove,
- and what the next level of the win might require.

So success sends you back into the loop.

A successful service may need a new Start: document the process.

That documentation may need to be Sustained: train the team and use the checklist.

That may create a new Succeed: the service can now be delivered without you doing every step.

One win can become the input for another cycle.

That is how the Power of NOW compounds.

Not because every action succeeds.

But because action creates information, information improves the next action, and successful actions can be turned into repeatable systems.

---

## The Evidence Behind Turning Goals Into Actions

The NOW Success Cycle is a practical framework, not a claim that researchers have scientifically proven these exact three labels.

But there is strong research behind one of its most important ideas:

> **A goal becomes easier to execute when the action is specified in advance.**

Psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed 94 independent tests of what are called **implementation intentions**—plans that specify when, where, or under what condition a person will act.

The basic structure is:

> **If situation Y occurs, then I will perform behavior X.**

Across those studies, implementation intentions had a medium-to-large positive effect on goal attainment. They were particularly useful for helping people **initiate goal-directed action** and protect ongoing goal pursuit from competing influences. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006)

That matters for this book because “I intend to succeed” is not an execution plan.

Neither is:

- “I need to market more.”
- “I should write.”
- “I have to follow up.”
- “I want to get healthier.”

Those are intentions.

An implementation intention sounds more like:

> **At 9:00 a.m., before I open email, I will contact the five prospects on my follow-up list.**

Or:

> **When I finish dinner on weekdays, I will write for twenty minutes before I turn on the television.**

Or:

> **If a new lead requests information, I will respond before the end of the business day.**

The research does not say that a plan makes success automatic.

It says that **pre-deciding the response can help close the gap between wanting to act and actually acting**.

That is a core Power of NOW principle:

> **Make the decision before the moment tries to renegotiate it.**

## Different Phases Need Different Questions

Once you see the cycle, you can stop asking one giant question like:

> “Why am I not succeeding?”

That question is too broad.

Ask the phase question instead.

### If you are in START:

- What is the first observable move?
- What can I do with the resources already in my hands?
- What am I waiting to know that I can learn only by moving?
- What would put this in motion today?

### If you are in SUSTAIN:

- Which action needs to repeat?
- What rhythm would make that realistic?
- What friction keeps breaking the pattern?
- What should be scheduled, templated, automated, delegated, or made easier?

### If you are in SUCCEED:

- What result proves this worked?
- What created that result?
- What must be protected?
- What should be repeated?
- What should become a process instead of a one-time win?

Those are smaller questions.

Smaller questions create more usable answers.

---

## Do Not Confuse Motion With Phase Progress

There is another trap.

You can be extremely active inside the wrong phase.

You can spend months “planning to start.”

You can keep redesigning the first step without ever taking it.

You can sustain an activity that is not producing the result.

You can experience success once and fail to capture what made it work.

That is why the cycle needs **evidence**.

At each phase, ask what would prove that you actually moved forward.

For Start, evidence might be:

- the email was sent,
- the page exists,
- the meeting was booked,
- the offer was published.

For Sustain:

- the action happened at the agreed rhythm,
- the streak continued,
- the process ran without being rebuilt from scratch.

For Succeed:

- the target result appeared,
- the metric moved,
- the deliverable was accepted,
- the customer renewed,
- the desired condition changed.

A phase without evidence can become a story you tell yourself.

Evidence keeps the cycle honest.

## The Point of the Cycle

The NOW Success Cycle is not meant to make life mechanical.

It is meant to make action legible.

When something feels stuck, you can diagnose the phase.

When something works, you can understand what to repeat.

When success feels far away, you can stop staring at the finish line and return your attention to the work the current phase requires.

Start.

Sustain.

Succeed.

Then learn from the result and begin the next cycle better than you began the last one.

In Chapter 4, we bring all of this down to the smallest useful planning horizon:

**today**.

You have defined the win.

You have separated needle-moving work from noise.

You have identified where your goals sit in the cycle.

Now you have to decide:

> **What deserves my NOW today?**
