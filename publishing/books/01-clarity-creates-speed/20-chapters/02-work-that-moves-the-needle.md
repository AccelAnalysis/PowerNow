# Chapter 2 — Work That Moves the Needle

There’s this story I can’t shake.

I was at lunch with a couple of friends, and one of them started talking about distraction—not in the “put your phone away” kind of way, but on a whole different level.

He had done a lot of mission work with Operation Blessing, traveling to places in Africa and other eastern countries through CBN and The 700 Club. Every time the team went overseas—to build something, feed people, evangelize, whatever the mission was—they never went alone. They always sent someone whose *only* job was to pray and intercede while everyone else did the hands-on work.

Picture that: one group mixing cement, serving food, talking with people. Another person, assigned entirely to fight what they believed was happening in the unseen realm while the visible work was going on.

On one of those trips, they brought a man from Ghana back to the United States to visit. At some point, somebody asked him:

> “Why don’t we see as much spiritual activity here as we do over there? Why does overseas feel so intense, and here it just feels… normal?”

The man from Ghana didn’t hesitate.

He said, “It’s not that there is less spiritual activity here. It’s that it manifests differently. Over there, you see it in more obvious ways. Here in the U.S., the spirits at work are the spirits of *busyness and distraction.* They operate by keeping everyone so distracted and so busy that you don’t realize what’s happening to you.”

Whether or not you share that exact spiritual framework, the description is dead on.

In our context, the “attack” doesn’t usually show up as some dramatic, obvious moment. It shows up as *distraction dressed up as productivity.* It shows up as you being constantly in motion—but rarely on the work that actually moves the needle. 

---

### Your Best Days Have a Pattern

If you look back at your *best* workdays—the days where you actually felt satisfied, not just tired—I’m willing to bet they had something in common.

For me, my most productive days haven’t been the ones where I checked 47 things off a list.

They’ve been the days when I:

* Knocked out the **top 2–4 items** that really mattered.
* Treated everything else as **“extra,”** not the main event.

I’ve tried everything over the years:

* Prioritized to-do lists
* The four-quadrant matrix of *urgent vs. important*
* Fancy task apps and color-coded systems

All of that can be helpful. But at the end of the day, the only question that really matters is:

> **Did I do the few things that actually move the needle?**

If I did, it was a meaningful day—even if I didn’t touch half my list.
If I didn’t, it was mostly noise—no matter how busy I felt.

This chapter is about that difference:

* The work that genuinely moves you forward
* The work that just *feels* like progress while it quietly siphons your time, energy, and “now” away 

Because if **The Power of NOW** is about anything, it’s about doing the things *now* that cause growth—and putting down the things that distract and slowly drain your execution capacity. 

---

### Not All Work Is Created Equal

Once you define what “success” looks like in this season (Chapter 1), a second truth hits you in the face:

> **Not all work is created equal.** 

Some tasks *look* productive. They keep you busy. They let you say, “Whew, I worked hard today.”

But if you step back and ask, *“Did that actually move anything important?”* the honest answer is often “Not really.”

On the other hand, there’s a much smaller set of actions that are disproportionately powerful—things like:

* Calls made
* Offers extended
* Proposals sent
* Content shipped
* Customers followed up with
* Systems built that remove ongoing friction from your business 

In almost every role and every business, a **small handful of actions drive the majority of meaningful results.**

Those are your *needle-movers*.

Everything else falls into one of two categories:

1. **Maintenance work** – necessary, but not powerful on its own.
2. **Noise** – activity that lets you feel productive without actually advancing the game.

This entire chapter—*“Work That Moves the Needle”*—is about learning to distinguish those categories and reorienting your “now” around the first one. 

---

### The Hidden Cost of Distraction (You Rarely Count It)

One of the most dangerous things about distraction is that you don’t feel the cost in the moment.

You don’t naturally think in terms of **opportunity cost**:

* When you choose an “easy” task, you almost never consciously acknowledge the *important* task you *didn’t* do.
* When you let your attention drift, you don’t see the compound effect of repeatedly delaying needle-moving work by a day, a week, a month.

You feel “busy.” You feel “tired.” But you may not feel “off track” until weeks or months later when you look up and realize:

> “I’ve been working so hard… and somehow I’m still not where I thought I’d be.”

If you believe, like I do, that we “wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers in high places,” then distraction stops being a minor annoyance and starts looking like a deliberate strategy.

But even if you strip all the spiritual language out of it, the pattern is the same:

* You *drift* instead of decide.
* You work hard on the wrong things.
* You never quite seem to make the jump you’re capable of.

Distraction is rarely about YouTube and TikTok alone.
More often, it’s about good-looking activities that quietly push the real work to “later.”

### When Learning the Tool *Is* the Work

Let me be honest about my own struggle here.

I’ve been in a season where I’m trying to build tools and systems that can unlock the next stage of growth in my business. The problem?

I’m **not** fully versed in those tools yet.

That means:

* Things take longer than they “should.”
* The learning curve feels like a tax on my time.
* I catch myself thinking, *“I’d rather just hire an expert to do this.”*

And truthfully? I *would* rather pay someone who’s amazing at it and reclaim that time for the work I enjoy most.

But when you’re trying to be a good steward of limited resources, you don’t always have that luxury **yet**.

So here’s the tension:

* Long-term, one of my first moves—as soon as I have the bandwidth and funding—is to **offload** certain tasks:

  * The ones that eat time I could spend on higher-value work.
  * The ones I don’t enjoy and therefore drag my feet on.

* Short-term, in this stage, the most important thing I can do is to move quickly **even on the things I don’t like.**

Because if I do them *now*:

* They leave my plate.
* They leave my mind.
* They leave my neck and shoulders.

They stop occupying mental space and emotional weight that I need for needle-moving work.

This is the heart of the book’s message:

> It’s not just about *doing* the work; it’s about doing the right work **now**—before distraction, fear, or fatigue gets a vote.

### Doing What You Don’t Like—Quickly

There’s a dangerous lie we tell ourselves:

> “If I don’t like doing it, I’ll get to it later when I have more time/energy/motivation.”

But “later” usually means “never” or “when it’s become urgent and painful.”

The paradox is this:

* The tasks you least enjoy are often the ones you **delay the most**,
* Which means they occupy **mind-space the longest**,
* Which means they are **the most expensive** in terms of mental bandwidth.

So in terms of *moving the needle*, one of the highest-leverage things you can do is:

> **Tackle the important, unpleasant thing *now*—before it grows teeth.**

No drama. No overthinking.
Just, “This has to get done. I don’t love it. But if I knock it out now, it stops taxing my brain for the next three weeks.”

That’s power.

Not “hype power,” not “motivational poster power,” but quiet, grown-up power.

The power of deciding, *“I will not allow my dislike of this task to steal my results.”*

### Simplify What Does Not Deserve a Decision

You do not need a celebrity story to understand this principle.

Every low-value choice that can be made once, standardized, delegated, or removed is one less place for your attention to leak.

I learned a version of this while working in economic development. An older gentleman on the Isle of Wight Economic Development team once said to me:

> “If you’ve got a good suit that works for you, wear it every day.”

He did not literally mean wear the same clothes until Human Resources stages an intervention.

He meant: **simplify the things that do not matter so you are less encumbered where it does matter.**

That can show up as:

- a standard proposal template instead of reinventing one every time,
- a fixed block for routine email,
- a default meeting length,
- a repeatable intake process,
- a small set of tools you know well,
- or a simple rule for what you will not work on during your best hours.

The purpose is not to automate your personality out of your life.

It is to stop spending premium attention on discount decisions.

### The Evidence Behind Focus

The cost of distraction is not just a motivational idea.

In a series of experiments published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer, and Jeffrey Evans found measurable **switching-time costs** when participants alternated between tasks. Those costs grew as the rules of the tasks became more complex. (Rubinstein, Meyer, & Evans, 2001)

That does not mean every task switch is catastrophic. Real work requires changing activities.

It does mean switching is not free.

Every time you interrupt one line of work to reorient to another, your brain has to recover the relevant rules, context, and next move. If your day is built around constant switching, some portion of your working time is spent repeatedly **getting back into the work**.

This is why clarity about needle-moving work creates speed in a second way:

> **It reduces how often low-value work is allowed to interrupt high-value work.**

You are not merely choosing better tasks.

You are protecting continuity around them.

### Tools, People, and the Power of NOW

Back in the concept of this book, we talked about how every major step-change in technology has been, at its core, about speed and leverage: typewriters over pen and paper, computers over typewriters, cell phones over pay phones. 

Each evolution gave people more **Power of NOW**:

* Faster communication
* Faster content creation
* Faster decision-making

AI is just the latest layer in that stack. It doesn’t remove the need for you to act—but it **amplifies what you can do in the same “now.”**

In the context of needle-moving work, that means:

* Use tools and tech to **shorten the distance** between idea and execution.
* Use subscriptions and services where it makes sense instead of insisting on doing *everything* yourself.
* Use people—contractors, team members, partners—to offload work that isn’t your highest contribution as soon as it’s financially wise to do so.

The key is this:

> **Tools and people don’t replace your responsibility. They multiply your obedience to “do it now.”**

They’re multipliers, not substitutes.

You still have to:

* Decide what the needle-moving work is.
* Commit to doing it sooner rather than later.
* Guard your time and attention so those things actually get done.

### A Simple Framework: Needle-Mover, Maintenance, Noise

To make this practical, here’s a simple way to classify your tasks.

Take your current to-do list and label each item as:

1. **Needle-Mover**

   * Directly advances revenue, impact, opportunity, or critical progress
   * Examples:

     * Sending a proposal
     * Making sales calls
     * Shipping content that leads to leads or sales
     * Completing a key system that will save you hours every week

2. **Maintenance**

   * Keeps the machine running, but doesn’t meaningfully advance it
   * Examples:

     * Inbox cleanup
     * Light admin
     * Routine reporting that nobody reads closely

3. **Noise**

   * Looks or feels productive but is mostly a way to avoid the hard stuff
   * Examples:

     * Endless redesigns of things that are already “good enough”
     * Low-impact meetings
     * “Research” that’s really just scrolling

Now ask:

> “If I could only complete *three* tasks today from this entire list, which ones would actually move the needle?”

That’s your **shortlist of high-leverage work**. Those are your *real* priorities.

Everything else becomes:

* “Nice if I get to it,” or
* “Delegate/automate,” or
* “Delete.”

### Bringing It Back to NOW

This book isn’t about shaming you for procrastination. It’s about **excellent, accelerated success**—the kind that comes from doing the right things, quickly and repeatedly. 

* It’s about realizing that unseen forces—spiritual, cultural, mental—are constantly trying to drag your “now” into distraction, busyness, and noise.
* It’s about becoming someone who sees through that fog and chooses needle-moving work on purpose.
* It’s about letting tools, technology, and other people *multiply* your execution instead of using them as excuses to delay it.

In the next chapter, we’ll map the work through the **NOW Success Cycle — Start → Sustain → Succeed** so you can tell what kind of execution problem you are actually solving. 

But none of that will matter if you don’t first decide:

> **“My ‘now’ belongs to the work that moves the needle—not to distraction dressed as productivity.”**

That decision, lived out one day at a time, is where your quicker, better success really begins.
