# Chapter 1 — Define the Win

I’d been in business for over a year and still had no idea what success looked like.

That was the embarrassing part.

The ironic part was that my business *was* about helping other people define and reach success in *their* ventures. On paper, I was a consultant. In reality, I was winging it with a nicer vocabulary.

Underneath the surface, here’s what was actually true:

* I had started an unscalable, *me-dependent* business. If I didn’t show up, nothing happened.
* There was no clear path to offload responsibilities. Everything bottlenecked at me.
* I didn’t have clean processes or operating procedures. Every project felt like reinvention.
* My business plan was half-written and definitely not fundable.
* I had no strategic plan. I was responding to life, not directing it.
* I had no CRM, so I couldn’t really leverage communication or historical data.
* I had “a sense of direction,” but I was basically rebuilding my offer from scratch with each client.
* And—classic mistake—I had let roughly 75% of my revenue come from one client.

Those weren’t little quirks. They were giant blind spots. The kind so big they don’t just block your line of sight—they *are* your windshield.

I couldn’t see the path ahead.

I felt it in my neck and shoulders. That heavy, tight ache that says, “You’re carrying a load you haven’t named yet.”

At some point, a question surfaced that I had managed to dodge for over a year:

> **“What, exactly, are you trying to build?”**

Not theoretically. Not eventually. Not “someday when this all settles down.”

Right now, with the life I had, the skills I had, the responsibilities I had:

**What was I actually trying to create?**

Was I building:

* A consulting agency?
* A marketing agency?
* A software company?
* Some Frankenstein combination of all three?

And a deeper question: **Was what I *wanted* even present in what I was *actually doing*?**

Up until that point, my real plan (even if I never said it out loud) was basically:

> “Say yes to good opportunities and see where this goes.”

That sounds open-minded and flexible. It’s actually how a lot of people end up somewhere they never meant to be.

---

## Drift is a decision… just not a conscious one

This isn’t about pretending you can know the future in perfect detail. You can’t. Businesses pivot. Markets change. You grow, your priorities shift.

But there’s a saying that punched me in the chest when I finally paid attention to it:

> **“If you aim at nothing, you’ll always miss most everything.”**

When you never define success, you don’t actually stay neutral. You **drift**.

You drift toward:

* Whoever shouts the loudest
* Whoever pays the soonest
* Whatever feels urgent instead of what’s actually important

And you drift **wherever the currents of the market take you**.

Here’s the dark little secret:

> For most people, those currents don’t take you *up*.
> They take you *under*.

I had to confront that. I had to admit that if I didn’t decide what “winning” looked like, I was authorizing everything around me to decide it for me—clients, bills, social media, random opportunities, other people’s emergencies.

So I started doing something basic and uncomfortable:

I *answered* the question.

---

## The moment I actually defined my win

When I finally sat down and got honest, here’s what came out.

I told myself:

> **“I want my business to be a platform that serves the business community and builds connections that actually lead to curated actions between people.”**

Not just inspiration. Not just information. **Action.** I wanted my work to create a bridge where businesses find each other and *do things* together—deals, projects, partnerships that turn into real money and real outcomes.

Then I got more specific:

* I had to **define my offerings** in no uncertain terms.
* I had to stop being a vague “consultant” and decide: *This* is what I do, *this* is who I do it for, and *this* is how it works.
* I had to say out loud, “I want my business to generate **$100,000 in monthly inflows**”—not because money is everything, but because saying a number forces you to get real about your model.

I also had to define my **life**, not just my income.

* What did I want my **day-to-day** to look like?
* How much time did I want with my family?
* How much margin did I want for serving others—not just with words, but with **funded deeds**?
* What actions did I want to be able to take when my business was healthy—scholarships, generosity, supporting causes I care about?

I had to write down the **actions** I wanted to be able to take with the bandwidth (financial and time) that excellence in my business would afford.

I had to define what “get there” even meant—for my passion, my pursuit, my family, my faith.

And I had to define my **tradeoffs**:

> I am willing, for a season, to trade sleep, money, extra activities—even some time with family—
> **so that later I can have all of those in multiples.**

At the same time, I had to mark off the things I **would not** put on the altar:

> I am *not* willing to sacrifice my faith, my conscience, or my integrity to obtain success.

Those two lists—what I would trade and what I would not—became a frame around my definition of “win.”

---

## Clarity comes *as* you write, not before

Here’s a crucial piece:

I didn’t wait until the whole thing was crystal clear in my head.

If I had, I’d still be waiting.

I discovered something important about how clarity works for me (and probably for you too):

> **It becomes clear *as* you write, not *before* you write.**

Most people are waiting for the “fully formed” idea before they commit anything to paper:

* the perfect vision
* the perfect plan
* the perfect mission statement

That’s a long, miserable wait.

I’m also a songwriter, and in songwriting, you learn this early:

You almost never know every stanza before you start. You catch one line, one melody, one fragment—and as you write, the song starts to **write itself**.

Defining your win works the same way.

When I first sat down to write my definition of success, it was fuzzy. But I started anyway:

* I wrote what I didn’t want.
* I wrote what I *thought* I might want.
* I wrote the half-sentences, the “I’m not sure but maybe it’s something like…” thoughts.

And somewhere in that unpolished mess, sentences started to sharpen. Priorities started to show up on the page. Non-negotiables rose to the surface. The real desires pushed past what sounded good and said, “No, actually, *this* is what you’re after.”

That’s why this chapter is called **Define the Win**, not **Wait Until the Win Feels Perfectly Defined in Your Mind**.

The “define” part is active. It’s a verb. It’s something you **do now**, not something you wait on until you feel smart enough, ready enough, or successful enough to write it. 

---

## Why we avoid defining success

If defining the win is so powerful, why don’t more people do it?

A few reasons:

### 1. Fear of being wrong

If you write down a clear definition of success, you might discover later that you want something different.

So what?

You revise it.

But the fear is real: “What if I climb the wrong mountain?” So instead, a lot of people don’t choose a mountain. They wander the valley and call it “keeping my options open.”

### 2. Fear of commitment

Clarity creates obligation.

When you say, “Success for me over the next three years looks like X,” you’ve just created a standard you can measure yourself against. That’s uncomfortable.

Vagueness is safer. If success is “be more successful,” then almost anything can be framed as “part of the process.”

### 3. Fear of exposure

A sharp definition of success forces you to see the gap between where you are and where you want to be.

That gap is humbling.

But ignoring it doesn’t make it smaller. It just makes you less equipped to cross it.

## What a real “win” actually includes

When I talk about “defining the win,” I don’t just mean “pick a revenue number.”

That’s part of it, but it’s not the whole thing.

A real definition of success has **layers**:

1. **The Work Itself**

   * What kind of work are you actually doing day to day?
   * Are you coaching, building, coding, teaching, selling, designing?
   * Do you want to be primarily a creator, an operator, a leader, a strategist?

2. **The Business Model**

   * Are you building a high-touch agency, a scalable software product, a solo advisory practice, a hybrid?
   * Will your income be driven by retainers, projects, subscriptions, product sales, licensing?

3. **Money and Metrics**

   * What specific numbers would tell you, “This is working”?
   * Revenue, profit, number of clients, average deal size, hours worked, etc.
   * For me, naming **$100,000 in monthly inflows** forced the question: *What model would make that realistic and healthy?*

4. **Your Day-to-Day Life**

   * What does an average Tuesday look like in your version of success?
   * When do you start work? When do you stop?
   * How often are you with your family and friends?
   * What kind of margin do you have for rest, health, and hobbies?

5. **Impact and Contribution**

   * Who is better off because your business exists?
   * How are you serving others in funded, tangible ways?
   * How are you using your surplus—time, money, skill—for more than just your own comfort?

6. **Non-Negotiables and Boundaries**

   * What are you unwilling to trade, even for big opportunities?
   * For me: my faith, my conscience, my integrity.

When you put those layers together, you don’t just have a wish list. You have a **definition**.

> “For the next 2–3 years, success looks like:
> this kind of work, in this kind of model, at this level of income, inside this kind of life, with this kind of impact—
> and **without** crossing these lines.”

That definition may evolve as you grow. But from where you stand right now, you need **something you can aim at**.

---

## Seasons of success, not a single forever-goal

One mistake people make is treating “success” like a single, final state you lock in for life.

That’s paralyzing.

Instead, think in **seasons**.

* What is a **win for this season**—the next 12–36 months?
* Given your current responsibilities, resources, and realities, what would “success” look like for *right now*?

You might be:

* in a **build season** (laying foundations, testing offers)
* in a **grow season** (refining a working model, scaling up)
* in a **harvest season** (reaping, consolidating, protecting what you’ve built)
* in a **reset season** (after a pivot, a crisis, or a major life change)

Each season needs its own definition of “win.”

The question isn’t “What do I want forever?” It’s:

> **“Given the life I actually have, what does a God-honoring, wise, hard-working *win* look like for this season?”**

When you answer that, even roughly, your actions can finally start to line up.

## The Evidence Behind a Clear Win

There is a reason vague goals feel harder to execute.

Psychologists Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent decades studying goal setting. Their work consistently found that **specific, challenging goals tend to produce better performance than vague instructions such as “do your best,”** when people are committed to the goal and have the ability and feedback needed to pursue it. Their research also identified several mechanisms: goals direct attention, mobilize effort, increase persistence, and encourage the development of strategies. (Locke & Latham, 2002)

That does not mean a highly specific goal magically guarantees success.

It means specificity gives your effort somewhere to go.

The same pattern appears in project research. Dov Dvir, Tzvi Raz, and Aaron Shenhar studied more than one hundred research-and-development projects and found that success was positively associated with the effort invested in defining requirements and technical specifications. More process for its own sake was not the point. **Clearer definition of what had to be delivered was.** (Dvir, Raz, & Shenhar, 2003)

That is the business-analyst version of this chapter:

> **Ambiguity creates rework. Definition reduces avoidable interpretation.**

The more often you must stop during execution to rediscover what you meant, what matters, or what “done” looks like, the more time you spend deciding instead of doing.

Defining the win moves some of those decisions **before** the execution window.

That is one of the ways clarity creates speed.

## How defining the win accelerates your “now”

You might be thinking, “This is all very reflective, but how does it help me move faster?”

Here’s how it connects to the whole point of this book—**quicker, better action**. 

When you define the win:

1. **Decisions speed up.**
   Every new request, opportunity, or idea can be held up to your definition:

   * “Does this move me toward my win for this season?”
   * If yes, it gets priority.
   * If no, it gets delayed, delegated, or deleted.

2. **Your “Big Three” become obvious.**
   It’s much easier to choose the three most important things for today (we’ll go deep on that in Chapter 4) when you know what game you’re actually playing. 

3. **You stop over-investing in the wrong things.**
   You might want to build scalable products, but if your current definition of success is “replace my job income within 12 months,” then maybe your short-term win is a handful of solid service clients, not a 2-year software build. Defining the win lets you be honest about that.

4. **You protect your execution capacity.**
   When you know your win, you can guard your energy, your time, and your attention from distractions that don’t merit your “now.” Later in the Power NOW series we’ll go deep on guarding your execution capacity—but it starts here, with clarity. 

Defining the win doesn’t guarantee things will be easy.

But it does mean you’re no longer burning precious energy debating what matters every 20 minutes. You’ve decided in advance. You can spend more of your willpower on **doing the work** instead of **arguing with yourself about which work to do**.

## Start before you feel ready

Let me say this plainly:

You will not feel “ready” to define your win.

You will feel:

* Underqualified
* Uncertain
* A little bit like a fraud
* Worried that you’ll change your mind later

And you probably *will* change your mind on some parts. That’s okay.

The goal is not to carve your success in stone. The goal is to **aim your life with more intention than the average person.**

You can adjust the aim as you learn.

What you can’t afford to do is live your whole life in a fuzzy, futureless existence where your days are full and your progress is vague.

You’re reading **Clarity Creates Speed**, the first book in the **Power NOW** series, for a reason. 

This is where that starts:

Not with a productivity hack.
Not with a new app.
Not with another planner.

It starts with you answering, in writing:

> **“What, exactly, am I trying to build in this season of my life?”**
