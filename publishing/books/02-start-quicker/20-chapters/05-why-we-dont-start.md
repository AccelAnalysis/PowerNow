# Chapter 5 – Why We Don’t Start

Book 1 helped answer the question:

> **What should I be doing now instead of everything else?**

You defined the win. You separated work that moves the needle from work that merely fills the day. You identified what deserves your attention.

That clarity matters.

But clarity creates a new problem.

You can know exactly what you should do—and still not do it.

You can know the customer needs to be invoiced.

You can know you need to follow up.

You can know the website needs to go live.

You can know you need to make the call, submit the proposal, write down the idea, start the business, finish the application, or begin the book.

You can even have a plan telling you exactly how to do it.

And nothing changes.

That is where **Start Quicker** begins.

Because knowing is not doing.

An idea is not an outcome.

A plan is not progress simply because it is well thought out.

An answer is not change.

We live in a time when getting answers is becoming easier and easier. Technology has progressively shortened the distance between a question and an answer. Search engines gave us information faster. Software made work faster. Smartphones put tools that once required an office into our pockets. And now artificial intelligence can help us think, draft, research, organize, analyze, and create at extraordinary speed.

Which means the important question is changing.

I said it recently this way:

> **The question is no longer, “Can you generate an answer?” It’s, “Can you effect change?”**

That distinction matters.

You can generate the perfect follow-up email.

But did the customer receive it?

You can generate a business plan.

But did anyone make the first sale?

You can generate a marketing strategy.

But did anything get published?

You can generate a proposal.

But did it get submitted?

You can generate fifty ideas before lunch.

But did any one of them become more real by dinner?

An answer describes what *could* happen.

Action begins changing what *is* happening.

And that is the gap we are going after in this book.

**Answer → Decision → Action → Changed Condition**

The invoice moves from **unsent** to **sent**.

The prospect moves from **someone I should call** to **someone I called**.

The idea moves from **something in my head** to **something that exists**.

The business moves from **something I have been talking about** to **something I have begun building**.

That is effecting change.

And before we learn how to make that transition faster, we need to understand why we so often stop before we cross it.

## When “Now” Feels Impossible

Right out of high school, I went to work at a call center.

It was not my dream job. I wasn't building toward some carefully designed career plan. I needed somewhere to make money.

I was on my own, and the place where I had previously worked lost its contract and shut down.

So I needed another job.

Now.

I took the call-center job and went to work.

Day after day.

And eventually I got to the point where I hated it.

I remember crying out to God:

> “God, I hate this job. Get me out of here.”

I wanted an exit.

What I heard on the inside instead was:

> **“Work harder.”**

That was not the answer I wanted.

I wasn't asking how to become better at the job.

I wanted out of the job.

But the instruction I felt was clear: **work harder.**

So I decided that when I went back to work, I was going to work as hard as I could.

I began taking initiative immediately on the things I could control—even though I still didn't like being there.

And somewhere in that period, I began applying what I now call the **Band-Aid Principle**.

You know how removing a Band-Aid works.

You can pull it slowly and spend a long time anticipating the pain.

Or you can pull it off and get it over with.

At work, I started doing that with the things I did not want to do.

Instead of letting the unpleasant task sit there all day while I thought about how much I didn't want to do it, I handled it.

Now.

Instead of letting the difficult thing contaminate the rest of the day with dread, I got it out of the way.

I didn't suddenly love the job.

That wasn't the point.

The rest of it simply became more tolerable because I stopped allowing the things I disliked most to follow me around mentally all day.

I kept doing that.

Not for one inspired afternoon.

For months.

Eventually, I was promoted into a floor-walker role, helping other people rather than spending all of my time doing the same work I had been doing on the phone.

It was different.

It was refreshing.

For a while.

Then I reached another point where I didn't like what I was doing.

And I went back to God:

> “Get me out of here. I don't like this.”

But before I could fully get the words out, I remembered what I had heard before.

> **Work harder.**

There was something being built in me that was bigger than that particular job.

The lesson was not:

> Stay forever in every situation you dislike.

And it certainly was not:

> If you're miserable, just work yourself into the ground.

The lesson was this:

> **My dislike of the present did not excuse me from acting in the present.**

I could want something different and still execute where I was.

I could have a vision for later and still take responsibility for now.

I could dislike the task and still do the task.

That matters because we often imagine that action becomes easy when circumstances become right.

When we have the right job.

The right amount of money.

The right team.

The right schedule.

The right opportunity.

The right feeling.

But much of the capacity we will need when the opportunity comes is built before the opportunity comes.

Doing things now is actually about the long term more than it is about the immediate.

Being able to stay long enough to realize success matters.

And staying is built, in part, through the ability to consistently execute.

> **Being able to act now means you will be around to act later.**

That is why starting matters so much.

# This Is Not a Book About Calling You a Procrastinator

It would be easy to reduce all of this to one word:

**Procrastination.**

“You know what you need to do and you're not doing it. Stop procrastinating.”

That is not particularly useful.

And it is not what this book is about.

*The Power of NOW* is about **harvesting**.

It is about putting the sickle in.

It is about doing the things now that can cause growth while putting down the things that distract you, drain you, and siphon away your productive energy.

There is something you can harvest today that may not be available forever.

A customer is ready now.

An opportunity is open now.

A relationship is warm now.

A market has a gap now.

You have the idea now.

Someone is willing to listen now.

The conditions do not have to be perfect for the opportunity to be real.

But opportunities often have a shelf life.

And there is an enormous difference between recognizing an opportunity and harvesting one.

I remember my brother talking about ideas he had.

Then later, there they were.

Someone else had done them.

Someone else had brought the idea into reality.

Someone else was reaping the rewards.

That is one of the painful things about ideas: having one does not reserve it for you.

The world does not issue intellectual claim tickets because you thought of something first.

Someone still has to move.

Someone still has to build.

Someone still has to ask.

Someone still has to make the call.

Someone still has to put the sickle in.

So when we talk about why we don't start, this is not an attempt to shame you.

It is an attempt to identify what is standing between you and the harvest.

Because different barriers require different responses.

If the real issue is fear, another to-do list will not solve it.

If the problem is friction, yelling at yourself to “be more disciplined” may accomplish very little.

If the problem is perfectionism, giving yourself more time may actually make the problem worse.

If the problem is your belief that later will somehow be easier, you can lose years waiting for a version of life that never arrives.

So let's name what is happening.

# The Four Forces Between Knowing and Doing

When you already know something deserves action but still aren't moving, four forces show up again and again:

1. **Fear**
2. **Friction**
3. **Perfectionism**
4. **The comfort of later**

Sometimes one dominates.

Sometimes all four are sitting in the same room.

The value of identifying them is not the label itself.

The value is precision.

Once you know what is stopping the start, you can stop fighting the wrong problem.

---

## 1. Fear: “What Happens If I Actually Do This?”

Fear rarely walks into the room and introduces itself properly.

It doesn't usually say:

> “Hello. I'm fear, and I'm stopping you.”

It sounds much more reasonable.

“I should think about this a little longer.”

“I don't want to rush.”

“I want to make sure I'm ready.”

“I probably need more information.”

“I should wait until the timing is better.”

Any of those things can be legitimate.

Discernment is real.

Preparation matters.

But we also have to be willing to tell ourselves the truth when preparation becomes protection.

What are we protecting ourselves from?

Sometimes failure.

> “What if I try and it doesn't work?”

Sometimes rejection.

> “What if I ask and they say no?”

Sometimes exposure.

> “What if I put this into the world and people judge it?”

Sometimes embarrassment.

> “What if everybody sees that I don't know as much as they thought?”

And sometimes success itself.

> “What if this actually works? What will be expected of me then?”

Success creates responsibility.

A successful offer has to be delivered.

A customer has to be served.

A growing business has to be managed.

An opportunity can expose the difference between dreaming about something and actually carrying it.

So we hesitate.

Because as long as the idea stays safely in our head, it cannot fail.

But it also cannot succeed.

Fear offers a strange bargain:

> **Avoid the short-term discomfort of finding out, in exchange for giving up the long-term possibility of finding out what could have happened.**

That's expensive.

---

## 2. Friction: “Why Does This Feel So Hard to Begin?”

You can want to do something and still encounter so much friction that beginning feels exhausting.

Maybe sending the invoice should take five minutes, but you have to find the hours, locate the billing information, remember the login, fix the template, find the customer's email, and then decide what to say.

Now a five-minute outcome has a forty-minute starting sequence attached to it.

Maybe you want to follow up with prospective customers, but the names are spread across email, text messages, your phone, sticky notes, and a spreadsheet you haven't opened in three weeks.

Maybe you want to build the website, but “build the website” exists in your head as one giant object.

Homepage.

Photos.

Logo.

Copy.

Services.

Domain.

Payments.

Policies.

SEO.

Contact form.

Suddenly you haven't even started, and you're already tired.

That is friction.

Some friction is physical.

Some is digital.

Some is procedural.

Some is informational.

And some comes from the way we have defined the task.

If “start a business” is sitting on your list as though it is one action, you haven't actually given yourself something to start.

You have given yourself a category.

Book 1 helped eliminate one major source of friction: **uncertainty about what matters**.

But even after you know what deserves your now, the path to touching it may still be tangled.

That matters.

Because sometimes the reason you haven't started is not that you don't care.

There are simply too many things between you and the first real move.

Chapter 6 will deal directly with that.

But first, there is another kind of friction that deserves its own attention.

---

# The Resource Mirage

One of the easiest ways to postpone action is to define the start in terms of resources you don't have.

“I would do it now, but I don't have enough money.”

“I don't have the staff.”

“I don't have the right software.”

“I don't have an office.”

“I don't know the right people.”

“I need an investor.”

“I need a larger audience.”

“I need more experience.”

Maybe you do need some of those things eventually.

But here's the question:

> **Do you need them to start—or do you need them to finish the version you are imagining?**

Those are not the same thing.

We often look at the fully built outcome and then price the resources required to create all of it.

Then we conclude:

> “I can't do it.”

But “I can't complete the whole thing today” and “I cannot move this at all today” are very different statements.

The better NOW questions are:

> **What can I do right now?**

> **What resources are already in my hands?**

> **How can I leverage the resources I have to activate resources I don't yet have?**

Maybe you don't have the money.

Do you have the ability to make the call that begins finding it?

Maybe you don't have the employee.

Do you know someone who can make an introduction?

Maybe you can't afford the final system.

Can you prove the process manually first?

Maybe you don't have an investor.

Can you get your first customer?

Maybe you don't know the answer.

Can you identify the person who does?

Maybe you don't have the entire afternoon.

Do you have ten minutes?

Resources create resources.

A relationship can create an introduction.

An introduction can create a meeting.

A meeting can create an opportunity.

An opportunity can create revenue.

Revenue can create capacity.

Capacity can create the thing that looked impossible when you were staring only at the resources you didn't have.

The goal is not to pretend constraints are imaginary.

Some constraints are very real.

The point is to stop confusing a constraint on the **whole journey** with a prohibition against taking the **next available step**.

Ask:

> **What is in my hand?**

That question has launched a lot more change than:

> “Why don't I have everything I need?”

---

## 3. Perfectionism: “It Has to Be Great Before It Can Be Real”

Perfectionism has excellent branding.

It sounds like quality.

It sounds like professionalism.

It sounds like high standards.

“I just want to get it right.”

“I don't want to put something half-baked into the world.”

“This represents me.”

“I only get one chance to make a first impression.”

There is truth in those statements.

Quality matters.

This book is not called *The Power of NOW: How Quicker Action Drives Success.*

It is about **quicker, better action**.

Better matters.

But quality and perfectionism are not the same thing.

Excellence asks:

> “How can I make this effective?”

Perfectionism often asks:

> “How can I eliminate every possibility that this exposes me to criticism?”

That is a very different standard.

The website does not have to contain everything you will ever want before anyone can visit it.

The business plan does not have to answer every question you will encounter over the next five years before you speak to your first customer.

The book does not have to emerge fully formed before you write its first honest paragraph.

The process does not have to be automated before you prove that it works.

And the first attempt does not have to look like the hundredth.

Here is the trap:

> **You cannot improve something that does not exist.**

“Better” requires something to make better.

You need the draft before you can refine the draft.

You need feedback before you can react to feedback.

You need the customer interaction before you can learn from the customer interaction.

You need the first version before there can be a second version.

Perfectionism wants version ten before it will authorize version one.

NOW says:

> **Create version one so version two becomes possible.**

We will do more with that idea later.

For now, simply notice whether your definition of “good enough to begin” has quietly become “impossible to begin.”

---

## 4. The Comfort of Later

This one may be the most dangerous because it doesn't feel dangerous.

Later feels responsible.

Later feels patient.

Later feels available.

“I'll do it tomorrow.”

“I'll make the call next week.”

“I'll work on the business when things calm down.”

“I'll focus on my health after this project.”

“I'll write the book when I have a free weekend.”

“I'll follow up with them when I have time to do it properly.”

Later offers you a future version of yourself who is wonderful.

Future You has more time.

Future You is more focused.

Future You has more money.

Future You isn't tired.

Future You doesn't have interruptions.

Future You apparently wakes up in a world where everybody else has stopped asking for things.

So Present You feels comfortable handing the responsibility over.

The problem is that when later arrives, it is no longer later.

It is now.

And Now You has many of the same pressures Present You had when you made the promise.

So you move it again.

Tomorrow becomes Friday.

Friday becomes next week.

Next week becomes next month.

Next month becomes “once we get through this quarter.”

And eventually you stop noticing how long you have been saying it.

This is how opportunities disappear without dramatic failure.

Nothing exploded.

Nobody told you no.

There was no catastrophic mistake.

You simply did not move while the opportunity was alive.

That is why NOW is about advantage.

Time matters.

Being earlier matters.

Responding while someone is interested matters.

Entering a market before it is crowded matters.

Capturing an idea before you forget it matters.

Making the introduction while the connection is warm matters.

Getting an offer into the world while someone needs it matters.

We often think risk lives in moving too quickly.

And it can.

But there is also risk in waiting.

Sometimes the biggest cost is not doing the wrong thing.

It is doing nothing while the window closes.

# The Band-Aid Principle

Let's return to the call center.

The Band-Aid Principle was not born because I had developed an advanced productivity system.

I discovered it because I was tired of spending more time dreading things than doing them.

Think about how often that happens.

A difficult phone call takes seven minutes.

You think about it for three days.

An email takes twelve minutes.

It occupies mental space all morning.

A conversation takes half an hour.

You rehearse it for two weeks.

An invoice takes five minutes.

You avoid looking at it every time you open your computer.

Avoidance doesn't eliminate the unpleasant thing.

It **extends your relationship with it**.

You experience it before you do it.

You experience it while you avoid it.

You experience it every time you remember you still haven't done it.

Then eventually you experience the task itself anyway.

That is a terrible deal.

The Band-Aid Principle says:

> **If the thing needs to be done and you already know it needs to be done, stop paying interest on the discomfort.**

Do the uncomfortable thing.

Now.

That doesn't mean recklessly acting on every thought that enters your head.

Book 1 gave us clarity precisely so we could distinguish what matters from what doesn't.

But once something has survived that test—once you know it is needle-moving work and you know it deserves your attention—resistance should not automatically get the deciding vote.

I did not have to like my call-center job to work.

I did not have to feel inspired to take initiative.

And you do not have to feel excited about the difficult conversation before you have it.

You do not have to feel confident before you ask.

You do not have to feel creative before you begin writing.

You do not have to feel ready before every legitimate first step.

Sometimes action comes after motivation.

But sometimes motivation shows up after action has already begun.

# Busy Is Not the Same as Started

There is another way we avoid starting that deserves attention.

We work.

A lot.

So much, in fact, that no one could reasonably accuse us of doing nothing.

Our calendars are full.

Our inboxes are moving.

We're answering people.

We're solving little problems.

We're attending meetings.

We're organizing things.

We're researching.

We're adjusting.

We're planning.

We're busy enough to feel exhausted.

But the thing that matters still hasn't moved.

This can be one of the hardest no-start patterns to detect because activity gives us cover.

We can say:

> “I didn't get to it. It was a crazy day.”

And it may have been.

But if every day is crazy and the needle-moving work is always the thing that gets pushed to tomorrow, the problem is no longer one crazy day.

It is a pattern.

This is another reason the Big Three matter.

They create a test.

At the end of the day, the question isn't merely:

> “Was I busy?”

It is:

> **“Did the things I said mattered actually move?”**

You can spend all day preparing to start.

You can organize the work.

Research the work.

Talk about the work.

Schedule the work.

Buy software for the work.

Watch videos about the work.

Ask AI questions about the work.

And still not do the work.

Which brings us back to the distinction at the beginning of this chapter.

# Generation Is Not Execution

Artificial intelligence makes this distinction impossible to ignore.

We'll go much deeper into AI, tools, and technology in Chapter 8.

But we need one principle now.

AI can help generate.

It can help you generate ideas.

Generate drafts.

Generate options.

Generate analysis.

Generate plans.

Generate code.

Generate language.

Generate possibilities.

That is extraordinary leverage.

It gives us more **Power of NOW** because things that used to take hours can happen in minutes, and things that took days can sometimes happen in hours.

But generation is not execution.

If AI writes the email and it remains on your screen, the customer received nothing.

If it creates the invoice language and no invoice is issued, your accounts receivable did not change.

If it builds the plan and nobody implements the plan, operations did not change.

If it creates the website code and the site never goes live, the market did not see it.

This is why I believe the question has changed:

> **The question is no longer, “Can you generate an answer?” It’s, “Can you effect change?”**

The value of an answer is ultimately realized through what the answer enables you to do.

That principle does not diminish AI.

It clarifies its power.

AI can collapse parts of the distance between idea and execution.

But you still have to cross the line into reality.

That is what Chapter 8 will help us accelerate.

Before technology can amplify your movement, though, you have to become willing to move.

Tools amplify execution.

They do not substitute for the decision to execute.

# The Cost of the Unacted-Upon Answer

There is a strange satisfaction in knowing the answer.

It can make us feel as though something has been accomplished.

We figured it out.

We solved it conceptually.

We made the plan.

We know what the next step is.

That *is* progress.

But it is dangerous when the emotional satisfaction of understanding becomes a substitute for implementation.

Because reality does not change based on how clearly you understand the move.

The unsent invoice is still unsent.

The unmade call is still unmade.

The unwritten idea can still disappear.

The unopened business can still produce zero revenue.

The unsubmitted proposal can still win zero work.

The unpublished website is invisible regardless of how good the copy is sitting on your laptop.

And sometimes, while we are thinking, somebody else is moving.

That doesn't mean you should race everybody.

It doesn't mean every idea must become a business.

It doesn't mean faster is automatically better.

It means that once you have identified something worth doing, **delay has a cost too**.

NOW is not about frantic movement.

NOW is about reducing unnecessary distance between the right decision and meaningful action.

# The Start Is Smaller Than the Success

Part of what keeps us frozen is that when we look at something we want to do, we see the entire finished outcome.

“Write the book.”

“Launch the business.”

“Fix the finances.”

“Build the website.”

“Create the program.”

“Go back to school.”

“Find customers.”

“Change careers.”

Those are destinations.

And destinations can be intimidating when your mind tries to execute all of them at once.

But success does not arrive in one action.

The NOW Success Cycle begins with **Start** for a reason.

Start is not Succeed.

You don't have to produce the entire harvest with the first swing of the sickle.

You just have to put the sickle in.

That distinction is where we are headed next.

In this chapter, I have intentionally not asked you to build the full action plan.

I haven't asked you to schedule an entire project.

I haven't asked you to solve every constraint you wrote down.

Why?

Because first we needed to expose what has been sitting between **knowing** and **doing**.

Now you have something much more useful than:

> “I just need to get motivated.”

You may be able to say:

> “I'm afraid of what happens if I ask.”

Or:

> “I've made the starting process unnecessarily complicated.”

Or:

> “I'm demanding a finished-quality version before I'll allow myself to produce the first one.”

Or:

> “I've been handing this to Future Me for six months.”

Or:

> “I've been looking only at the resources I don't have instead of what I can do with what I already have.”

That is progress.

Because you cannot design a better start until you understand what has been stopping this one.

# You Don't Have to Feel Ready

If there is one thing I want you to carry out of this chapter, it is this:

> **You do not have to eliminate resistance before you begin.**

Fear may still be there.

The task may still feel uncomfortable.

You may still wish you had more resources.

You may still want the first version to be better than it is going to be.

Part of you may still prefer tomorrow.

The point is not to wait until all of those voices disappear.

The point is to stop automatically obeying them.

That lesson goes all the way back to that call center.

I didn't need to love where I was to take responsibility for what I could do there.

And sometimes the most important question isn't:

> “How do I get myself to feel differently?”

It is:

> **“What can I do from here?”**

From this job.

From this budget.

From this level of experience.

From this amount of time.

From this network.

From this messy first draft.

From this uncomfortable situation.

From here.

Now.

That question creates movement because it stops negotiating with an imaginary starting point.

You don't start from where you wish you were.

You start from where you are.
