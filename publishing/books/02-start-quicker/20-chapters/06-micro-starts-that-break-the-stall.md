# Chapter 6 — Micro-Starts That Break the Stall

Opening the day, everything stayed on the to-do list.

I remember populating the to-do app on my phone with all the things that were on my plate—all the things that were in front of me.

At first, it felt good.

There is something satisfying about getting everything out of your head and putting it somewhere you can see it. I could organize it. Prioritize it. Put the different pieces in order. I started adding steps and then sub-steps beneath those steps.

Call this person.  
Update that document.  
Create the new offer.  
Follow up with that customer.  
Work on the website.  
Outline the new system.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

The more I added, the more it felt like I was taking control.

And there is nothing wrong with writing things down. In fact, there is something vitally important about it. If the vision is never made plain, it is hard for anybody to run with it—including you. If you are leading other people, eventually somebody else may need to understand the process too.

So getting it out of my head was good.

The problem showed up the next day.

I opened the app to get started, and what had felt like organization the night before suddenly felt like a wall.

There were tasks.

There were subtasks.

There were priorities inside of priorities.

Everything was visible at once.

My organization had quietly become a kind of **paralyzing control center**.

I remember beginning to work on something that was genuinely important, but another item caught my attention.

*Maybe I should do that first.*

So I moved.

Then something else surfaced.

*I forgot about that.*

Then another.

Before long, I had spent the day moving between things without really moving any one thing enough.

All I know is that when the day ended, the list had not gotten smaller.

It had gotten bigger.

The activities of that day had produced new follow-ups, new ideas, new problems and new things that needed attention. Yesterday's list was still there, and today's life had added more to it.

Yesterday's unfinished work plus today's incoming work became tomorrow's avalanche.

Can you see the snowball effect?

It isn't always that you don't know what needs to be done.

Sometimes you know **too much of what needs to be done all at once**.

And there is a major difference between knowing everything that needs to happen and knowing what to do with your hands **right now**.

That is where micro-starts come in.

---

## Inventory Is Not Instruction

The list wasn't the problem.

The mistake was expecting the list to do something it wasn't designed to do.

A master to-do list is an **inventory of work**.

It can tell you what exists.

It can remind you what needs attention.

It can show you how much is in front of you.

But an inventory doesn't necessarily tell you:

> **Where do I put my hands first?**

That is a different question.

In Book 1, *Clarity Creates Speed*, we worked on identifying what matters. You defined the win. You separated needle-moving work from busywork. You narrowed the day down instead of treating every task like it deserved equal weight.

But even after you have decided what matters, you can still look at one important item and freeze.

“Build the website.”

Okay.

Where do I put my hands?

“Write the book.”

Okay.

Where do I put my hands?

“Launch the business.”

Okay.

Where do I put my hands?

“Fix my finances.”

Okay.

Where do I put my hands?

That is the problem Chapter 6 is designed to solve.

Because sometimes the work is not impossible.

The **start** is simply too large.

Or too vague.

Or too far away from where you actually are.

You are looking at the whole staircase when all you need to know is where to place your foot.

---

## Shrink the Start, Not the Goal

Let me make an important distinction.

Micro-starting is **not thinking small**.

I am not telling you to reduce your ambition.

I am telling you to reduce the size of the entrance.

There is a difference.

If your goal is to write a book, the answer is not:

“Well, I guess I'll just write a paragraph and call that a book.”

No.

The goal can remain:

**Write the book.**

But the starting requirement might become:

**Write the first paragraph.**

The destination did not shrink.

The entrance did.

If you need to launch a website, the goal can remain:

**Launch the website.**

But the first move might be:

**Write the headline people will see when they land there.**

If you need to follow up with 40 customers, the goal can remain:

**Re-engage the customer list.**

But the first move might be:

**Write the first follow-up message to one customer.**

If you need to get your business finances under control, the goal can remain:

**Build a clear financial picture of the business.**

But the first move might be:

**Write down the current bank balance and the next three bills due.**

That's the principle:

> **Shrink the start, not the goal.**

Big goals are allowed.

Big visions are allowed.

Big assignments are allowed.

But you do not have to lift the full weight of the finished vision in order to begin moving toward it.

---

## What a Micro-Start Actually Is

A **micro-start** is:

> **The smallest, clearest meaningful action you can take right now that touches the real work and moves it out of zero.**

Every word in that definition matters.

**Smallest** means you have removed unnecessary weight from the start.

**Clearest** means you know what your hands are actually supposed to do.

**Meaningful** means this is not random activity that merely makes you feel productive.

**Right now** means it does not depend on some imaginary future version of your schedule.

**Touches the real work** means you are no longer preparing to prepare.

And **moves it out of zero** may be the most important part of all.

Zero is a special place.

At zero, there is nothing to edit.

Nothing to improve.

Nothing to send.

Nothing to respond to.

Nothing to test.

Nothing to build upon.

The first rough paragraph may not be good—but now a paragraph exists.

The first sales message may need improvement—but now a message exists.

The first offer may need refinement—but now an offer exists.

The first invoice may still need another detail—but now an invoice exists.

You have moved from **nothing** to **something**.

From zero to one.

And one can be improved.

One can be multiplied.

One can be edited.

One can become two.

But you have to leave zero first.

That is the job of the micro-start.

## Find the First Physical Move

One reason tasks remain intimidating is that we describe them at too high a level.

Consider:

**Work on marketing.**

What does that mean?

Your hands cannot “work on marketing.”

Your hands can:

- write three possible headlines;
- call a former customer;
- draft a short offer;
- record a 60-second video;
- send a follow-up message.

Those are actions.

Or:

**Get the business organized.**

Your hands cannot “get organized.”

They can:

- create the client folder;
- write the first three steps of the intake process;
- enter one customer into the system;
- put the next invoice date on the calendar.

Or:

**Write my book.**

Your hands cannot physically “write the book” in one moment.

They can:

- write the working title;
- list five things you know you need to say;
- write the opening sentence;
- write the first paragraph.

When you are stalled, listen carefully to the language you are using.

Words like:

*work on,*  
*figure out,*  
*deal with,*  
*get started on,*  
*handle,*  
*look into*

can hide the actual action.

Ask instead:

> **If somebody watched me do the first move, what would they physically see me doing?**

Writing?

Calling?

Typing?

Sending?

Opening?

Listing?

Asking?

Choosing?

Entering?

Uploading?

Those verbs get closer to the start line.

“Build my website” is a project.

“Write three possible homepage headlines” is a start.

“Improve sales” is a goal.

“Write the name of the first prospect I will contact and the opening line I will send” is a start.

“Start a business” is a vision.

“Write what I will sell, who I will sell it to, and what I will ask the first customer to do” is a start.

The more physical and observable the action becomes, the less room there is for confusion to hide.

## Small Is Not the Point. Movement Is.

There is a trap here.

Once people hear “micro-start,” they can become very good at doing tiny things that don't matter.

You can spend five minutes rearranging the icons on your phone.

You can spend seven minutes changing the name of a folder.

You can spend ten minutes researching another productivity app.

All of those are small.

None of them necessarily moves the thing that matters.

So don't confuse **small activity** with a **micro-start**.

A true micro-start passes three tests:

### 1. It touches the real work.

If the goal is generating revenue and you already have a finished offer, adjusting your logo probably isn't the start.

Contacting the first qualified person might be.

### 2. It changes something.

When you finish, something should exist, be decided, be initiated or be different.

A name is on the page.

A first draft exists.

A customer has been contacted.

A question has been asked.

A number has been faced.

### 3. It makes the next move easier to see.

A useful micro-start opens the road.

Once you write the first paragraph, the second paragraph becomes easier to see.

Once you call the first person, you learn something that helps with the second call.

Once you write the offer, you can see what needs clarification.

The micro-start is not meant to become your permanent level of effort.

It is a **launch point**.

The goal is not to stay tiny.

The goal is to get moving.

---

## Don't Wait for Resources You Don't Need Yet

Sometimes the size of the start is hiding inside a resource excuse.

“I don't have enough money.”

“I don't have a team.”

“I don't have the right software.”

“I don't know the right people.”

Sometimes those are legitimate limitations.

Resources matter.

But the better question is the one at the heart of this entire series:

> **What do I have in my hands right now, and what can I do with it?**

Maybe you cannot fund the entire idea today.

Can you write the one-page concept?

Can you identify the first person you need to talk to?

Can you ask for an introduction?

Can you create a simple version that proves the idea?

Can you draft the proposal that could activate somebody else's resources?

Can you make the first sale before building everything else?

The micro-start does not require pretending that missing resources don't matter.

It asks something more useful:

> **What can I do now that changes my access to what I need next?**

You may not have the money.

But you may have the idea.

You may have a relationship.

You may have a phone.

You may have expertise.

You may have five customers who already trust you.

You may have a computer.

You may have somebody who knows somebody.

You may have the ability to ask.

Start with what is actually in your hand.

Use one resource to activate another.

That is far more powerful than sitting still because you cannot see the entire path yet.

## Every Restart Is a Start

There is another part of starting that doesn't get talked about enough.

We tend to imagine “starting” as something that happens once.

The business starts.

The book starts.

The project starts.

The new habit starts.

But life does not usually work that neatly.

You start Monday morning.

Then you get interrupted.

You start again after lunch.

You have a great week.

Then something happens and you lose three days.

You start again.

You become discouraged.

You question the whole thing.

Then you show up again.

That is another start.

My pastor has said something over the years that has stayed with me. He talked about times when he felt like quitting and about the voice on the inside that kept bringing him back:

> “It doesn't matter how many times you quit, as long as you show up for work.”

I don't take that to mean quitting doesn't matter.

I take it to mean **returning matters more than the moment you wanted to quit**.

Every time he showed up, he made another decision.

*I'm here.*

*I'm doing this again.*

*Let's go.*

That is powerful because sometimes we put too much significance on the moment when we lost momentum.

“I missed yesterday.”

“I stopped for a month.”

“I got discouraged.”

“I walked away from it.”

Okay.

What are you doing **now**?

You do not only need to become good at starting.

You need to become good at **restarting quickly**.

The interruption does not have to become abandonment.

The bad day does not have to become a bad month.

The missed opportunity does not require you to miss the next one.

A restart can be a micro-start too.

Open the file again.

Make the next call.

Write the next sentence.

Return the message.

Show up for work.

You don't need to recreate all the enthusiasm you had at the beginning.

You need a decision.

And sometimes that decision is only:

> **I'm here again.**

## You Don't Need the Whole Staircase

There is another way we accidentally make micro-starts heavy.

We try to map every micro-start before taking the first one.

If the goal is writing a book, you could sit down and develop a beautiful 127-step system:

Research.

Brainstorm.

Outline.

Sub-outline.

Draft.

Edit.

Review.

Format.

Design.

Publish.

Market.

And on and on.

Soon your micro-start plan becomes another giant list staring back at you.

You are back where this chapter began.

You don't need the whole staircase.

You need the **next visible step**.

Take it.

Then look again.

The next move may become obvious because of what the first move taught you.

That matters.

Action produces information that thinking alone cannot always give you.

You write the first paragraph and realize the story should begin somewhere else.

Good.

You call the first customer and discover the objection you need to address.

Good.

You put the offer on paper and realize you cannot clearly explain the value yet.

Good.

You started.

Now you have something real to respond to.

The sequence can be:

**Start → See → Start again.**

Not:

**Predict everything → organize everything → understand everything → maybe start.**

You only need enough clarity for the move in front of you.

## When a Small Start Still Isn't Enough

There is one more problem.

Sometimes you can identify the micro-start perfectly.

It is small.

It is clear.

It takes two minutes.

You know exactly what your hands need to do.

And you still don't do it.

That tells us something.

The problem is no longer that the first step is too big.

You may need something else.

A clock.

A deadline.

A rule.

A promise.

Another person who knows what you said you were going to do.

A condition that makes continuing to negotiate with yourself harder than simply moving.

In other words, sometimes the start does not need to get smaller.

Sometimes you need **pressure**.

Not panic.

Not chaos.

Not somebody beating you down.

**Productive pressure.**

A designed constraint that helps turn what you intend to do into what you actually do.

That's where we're going next.

But before you turn the page, look again at what you wrote in this chapter.

At the beginning, it was something sitting on a list.

Now it has a first move.

Maybe even a first piece.

That is what a micro-start does.

It does not finish the harvest.

It puts the sickle in.

And once the sickle is in your hand, the question changes.

Not:

**“Will I ever start?”**

But:

**“How do I make sure I move when it's time to move?”**

That's Chapter 7.
